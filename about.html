Deaths on Colorado's Fourteeners (14er)

This project

Colorado’s Fourteeners—peaks rising above 14,000 feet—are among the most sought-after mountains in the country and attract hundreds of thousands of hiker use days each year. The Colorado Fourteeners Initiative estimates that 256,000 hiker use days were recorded across the state’s 14ers in 2025, with more than 400,000 hiker use days recorded during the peak of the pandemic in 2020.

With so many people venturing into Colorado’s high country, accidents and fatalities have unfortunately become part of the history of these mountains. Yet there has historically been no single, comprehensive public record of fatalities on Colorado’s Fourteeners. Previous efforts have documented portions of this history. For example, a 2010–2017 analysis identified 57 deaths, but its author noted that the information was incomplete and that some incidents may have gone unreported.

Deaths on Colorado’s Fourteeners was created to help fill that gap.

This site documents reported fatalities on Colorado’s fourteen-thousand-foot peaks, including incidents involving falls, rockfall, avalanches, exposure, medical events, lightning, and other causes. The goal is not simply to count deaths, but to preserve the stories, circumstances, and history surrounding these incidents and the people who lost their lives.

The database combines information from publicly available sources with crowdsourced contributions from the 14er community. Whenever possible, incidents are cross-referenced and independently verified. Conflicting information is reviewed, duplicate reports are consolidated, and records are updated as additional information becomes available.

Because historical records are often incomplete, this database should not be considered a definitive or official count of fatalities. Some incidents may have received little or no public coverage, while details surrounding others may remain uncertain. The database is intended to be a living historical resource—one that can be corrected, expanded, and improved as new information comes to light.

Above all, this project is intended to document these events with respect for the people who died, their families, and the mountains themselves.

Animal emergency hospitals

Backcountry injuries, heat illness, trauma and snakebites can become life-threatening quickly. The resources below include 24-hour emergency hospitals plus mountain-area clinics with after-hours or on-call coverage.

Rattlesnake bite: take a photo of the snake

The following applies to all rattlesnake bites

My dog Loki was bitten by a rattlesnake, a Mojave Sidewinder, so I'm adding this information in the event someone else experiences a similar situation.

  • Take a photo of the snake
  • Call the nearest emergency hospital and make sure they have antivenom available.
  • Send the photo of the snake to the hospital for identification.
  • Go to the hospital -- immediatly,

Rattlesnakes have different venom which is important to identify for proper treatment. Pass that photo to the emergency veterinary hospital for proper identification and treatment.

Treat every suspected rattlesnake bite as an emergency. If it is safely possible, take a photo of the snake from a distance without approaching, cornering, catching or killing it. Call the nearest emergency veterinary hospital while you begin transport and ask whether rattlesnake antivenom is currently in stock. If it is not, ask where they recommend you go. Do not delay departure waiting for a callback.

  • Keep your pet as quiet as possible; carry them rather than letting them walk if you can do so safely.
  • Do not cut the bite, attempt to suck out venom, or apply a tourniquet.
  • If you cannot reach a hospital by phone, head toward the nearest open emergency veterinary facility immediately.

Colorado 14er Climbing Resources

Plan well. Climb within your limits. Come home.

No Colorado 14er is truly “easy.” High altitude, fast-changing weather, snow, loose rock, route-finding and fatigue can turn a straightforward hike into an emergency. The resources below are intended to help climbers choose appropriate routes, prepare for changing conditions and make conservative decisions.

The summit is optional. Turning around is a successful safety decision.

Before every climb: study the route, check recent conditions and trailhead access, review a mountain-specific weather forecast, set a turnaround time, tell someone your plan, and carry enough equipment to survive an unexpected delay or night out.

Best practices

Choose the route — not just the mountain

Class • distance • gain • exposure • rockfall • route-finding • commitment

Read the complete route description and recent condition reports. A peak can have both an easy standard route and much more serious alternatives. Check the route’s difficulty and risk factors, not merely the summit name.

Use a mountain-specific NWS point forecast and review radar before leaving. If thunderstorms are forecast, scale back or choose another day. Set a turnaround time that gets you off exposed ridges before storms develop. If you hear thunder, move quickly toward lower, safer terrain.

Use redundant navigation

Offline map + GPX + backup + route knowledge

Download maps and the route before leaving cell service. Know the major landmarks and where mistakes commonly occur. A phone is useful, but batteries die and screens break; carry backup power and consider a paper topo/compass for remote or complex routes.

Carry the Ten Essentials

Prepare to be delayed — even on a day hike

Navigation, headlamp, sun protection, first aid, knife/repair kit, fire, emergency shelter, extra food, extra water and extra clothing belong in a 14er pack. A windproof emergency bivy or shelter offers more protection than a thin foil blanket alone.

Ten Essentials

Hydration: enough, but not forced

Drink to thirst • carry reserve • include electrolytes/food on long days

Sodium matters because it helps regulate fluid balance and normal nerve and muscle function, and sweat contains sodium. On long or hot efforts, salty foods or electrolyte drinks can help replace losses. But salt does not make overdrinking safe: excessive plain water or sports drink can contribute to exercise-associated hyponatremia. Drink according to thirst rather than forcing a fixed amount.

WaterElectrolytesAvoid overdrinking

Fuel the climb

Carbohydrates during • protein for satiety/recovery • extra food for delays

Carbohydrates are convenient fuel during sustained climbing: fruit, bars, chews, tortillas, pretzels and similar foods are easy to eat while moving. Protein is useful for satiety and recovery but is not a substitute for carbohydrate during a hard endurance day. Bring food you already know you tolerate at altitude.

CarbohydratesProteinExtra calories

Respect altitude

Acclimatize • recognize symptoms • descent is treatment

Fitness does not make someone immune to altitude illness. Headache, nausea, unusual fatigue and dizziness after ascent can be warning signs. Do not keep ascending when symptoms are worsening. Confusion, loss of coordination or severe shortness of breath are emergencies and require immediate descent and medical help.

Snow changes the mountain

Avalanche forecast • traction • axe skills • seasonal route choice

A Class 1 summer trail can become serious mountaineering terrain under snow. When snow is present, check the Colorado Avalanche Information Center. Crampons, an ice axe and avalanche equipment only help when you have the training to use them correctly.

Route • vehicle • companions • return time • emergency contact

Static rope vs. dynamic climbing rope

Rope type What it does Typical uses Critical limitation
Dynamic climbing rope Designed to stretch under impact and absorb energy from a climbing fall. Lead climbing, sport, trad, alpine climbing and other systems where a climber may fall onto the rope. Use a rope certified for the intended climbing system and a compatible belay device. Training is essential.
Static / low-stretch rope Designed to minimize stretch while loaded. Hauling, fixed lines, rope access, rescue systems and certain rappelling applications. Do not use static rope to catch a lead-climbing fall. The lack of dynamic stretch can transmit extremely high forces to the climber and system.
14er context: Most standard Class 1 and Class 2 routes do not require a rope. Carrying a rope without knowing anchors, knots, belaying, rappelling and rescue systems can create additional hazards. Seek qualified instruction before using ropes on technical 14er terrain.

Good first 14ers

“Beginner” is relative. These routes are among the least technical standard 14er routes in dry summer conditions. They still require fitness, high-altitude judgment, weather awareness and the ability to turn around.

Mount Sherman

One of the shortest standard 14er hikes. The route uses old mining roads and trail. Road access can be rough; use the recommended Fourmile Creek approach and respect private-property boundaries.

Better choices for experienced hiking families

Family-friendly does not mean risk-free. Children are susceptible to altitude illness too. Choose a route based on the individual child’s hiking experience, fitness and ability to communicate symptoms — not age alone. Have a low threshold to turn around.

Mount Bierstadt — West Slopes

Best all-around access

Paved access, restrooms at Guanella Pass, a recognizable trail and moderate mileage make Bierstadt a reasonable first family 14er for strong hikers. Expect wet boardwalk/willow areas, a long climb and a boulder-hopping summit finish.

Mount Sherman — Southwest Ridge

Shorter distance and elevation gain

The standard route is short by 14er standards, which reduces the length of the day. Keep children away from abandoned mine structures and openings, stay on the established route and plan carefully for the rough access road.

Quandary Peak — East Ridge

Obvious trail, but sustained climbing

The route is easy to follow and has low exposure, but it is steep and crowded. Families should be comfortable with several hours of continuous climbing and descending, and should keep clear of mountain goats and other wildlife.

Grays Peak — North Slopes

Class 1 trail with more mileage

A good progression after shorter high-altitude hikes. The trail itself is straightforward, but 7.5 miles and 3,000 feet of gain are a substantial day for a child. Do not automatically add Torreys just because it is nearby.

Better 14er choices for experienced mountain dogs

New to 14ers? Leave the dog home for your first few climbs. First learn how you personally handle altitude, weather, route-finding and long descents. When your dog is ready, use a leash, carry extra water and food, protect paws from sharp talus, pack out waste and have a realistic plan to carry or evacuate an injured dog. Always verify current land-manager rules before the hike.

Mount Bierstadt — West Slopes

Dogs allowed • hand-held leash required in Mount Evans Wilderness

A common dog-friendly 14er for fit, experienced mountain dogs in dry summer conditions. The final boulder field can be hard on paws and may require assistance. Do not take a dog across the Sawtooth.

Wilderness Rules

Quandary Peak — East Ridge

Common dog route • crowded • mountain goats

The Class 1 standard route is more dog-compatible than technical 14ers, but crowds, sharp rock and frequent wildlife encounters make a leash especially important. Confirm current Summit County/Forest Service regulations before the trip.

Mount Sherman — Southwest Ridge

Shorter route • mining terrain • rocky upper mountain

A shorter 14er can reduce total time on a dog’s feet, but old mining areas and sharp rock require control and paw awareness. Keep dogs away from mine openings and structures.

Grays Peak — North Slopes

Class 1 • longer rocky alpine day

The established Class 1 trail is preferable to scrambling terrain, but the mileage, altitude, sun and rough rock still demand a conditioned dog. Carry enough water for both of you; do not assume reliable natural water is available or safe.

Worst practices — things not to do

Treat an “easy 14er” as an easy hike.

Class 1 describes the technical terrain, not the altitude, weather, physical effort or consequences of being unprepared.

Start late with thunderstorms in the forecast.

Do not plan to “beat” lightning. If the forecast does not provide a reasonable weather window, choose another objective or another day.

Keep climbing after thunder begins.

The summit is not shelter. Peaks and ridgelines are among the places you do not want to be during an electrical storm.

Rely on one phone app and cell service.

Download maps in advance, carry backup power and know the route. Electronics can fail at the exact moment navigation becomes difficult.

Leave the headlamp and emergency shelter in the car.

Injury, route-finding problems or weather can turn a morning hike into an unplanned night above treeline.

Force large amounts of water “to stay ahead of dehydration.”

Overdrinking during prolonged exertion can be dangerous. Drink to thirst and replace food/electrolytes sensibly rather than chasing an arbitrary fluid target.

Ignore altitude symptoms because you are fit.

Fitness does not prevent altitude illness. Worsening symptoms, confusion, poor coordination or severe breathing difficulty are reasons to descend, not push for the summit.

Carry technical gear you do not know how to use.

An ice axe, crampons or rope can create false confidence. Get hands-on instruction and practice before relying on technical systems in consequential terrain.

Use static rope to catch a climbing fall.

Static/low-stretch rope is not a substitute for certified dynamic climbing rope when fall arrest is part of the system.

Separate the group and leave someone behind.

A tired or symptomatic partner is often the person most likely to need help. Adjust pace or turn the group around rather than abandoning them.

Cut switchbacks or follow random cairns off-route.

Shortcuts accelerate erosion and can lead into dangerous terrain. Stay on sustainable established routes and verify navigation rather than blindly following footprints or cairns.

Let a dog roam around wildlife or exposed terrain.

A loose dog can chase goats, enter hazardous terrain, injure wildlife or become an evacuation problem. Use a leash where required and whenever conditions make control important.

Assume rescue will be quick.

Colorado SAR teams are exceptionally capable, but mountain rescues can take hours. Carry enough clothing, shelter, food and first aid to remain stable while help is mobilized.

Park illegally or ignore access/private-property restrictions.

Blocked roads can delay emergency vehicles and threaten continued public access. Check current trailhead rules, seasonal closures and landowner restrictions before departure.

Conditions change. Route ratings above describe standard routes in generally dry summer conditions. Snow, ice, smoke, wind, storms, closures, road damage and access rules can substantially change difficulty. Always verify current information immediately before a climb.
